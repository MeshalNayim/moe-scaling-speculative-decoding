# Part 2.3 — Why MoE?

DeepSeek-V3 stores 671B parameters but only 37B fire for any single token (1 shared expert + top-8 of 256 routed experts, on top of the always-on MLA attention block). Llama-3 8B is a useful counterfactual: a dense model where every one of its ~8B parameters touches every token. Comparing the two clarifies what MoE is buying and at what cost.

## Total vs. activated parameters and what that does to cost

The headline trade is that MoE decouples the *parameter budget* from the *per-token compute budget*. DeepSeek-V3 has roughly 84× more total parameters than Llama-3 8B but only ~4.6× more activated parameters per token. Training FLOPs follow activated parameters (the un-fired experts don't contribute), so DeepSeek-V3 trains at a per-token FLOPs cost much closer to a 37B dense model than to a 671B dense model. Memory does *not* follow activated parameters: every byte of weight has to be physically resident somewhere, so the 671B total drives sharding requirements. That mismatch — "expensive to store, cheap to run" — is the whole reason TP/EP infrastructure exists. Without distribution, a dense 671B model would need ~1.3 TB at bf16 just for weights and no single accelerator could hold it; the MoE version is exactly as hard on memory but lets you scale compute by adding more experts rather than by making the active path more expensive.

## Communication and how it scales with `num_experts` and `topk`

The MoE block doesn't come for free in distributed systems. Expert parallel ships every routed token to the rank that owns its assigned expert and ships the result back — two all-to-all collectives per topk slot per layer. The bandwidth per layer scales linearly with topk (more slots means more shipments) and roughly linearly with batch (more tokens to route). The number of experts doesn't directly grow per-token traffic — increasing `n_routed_experts` from 64 to 256 doesn't make each token's data any bigger — but it does grow the *fan-out* of every all-to-all, which makes the collective harder to schedule efficiently and amplifies any topology mismatch between the routing distribution and the physical network. The naive failure mode is "all tokens want expert 7"; then rank 7 receives the whole batch while other ranks idle. DeepSeek-V3 mitigates this with `n_group=8` groups and a topk-group routing constraint, plus an auxiliary load-balancing loss, both designed to spread the all-to-all traffic uniformly across destination ranks.

## Inference economics

At low load, MoE is dramatically cheaper than its parameter-equivalent dense model: per-token FLOPs scale with activated parameters, so DeepSeek-V3 serves a token roughly as cheaply as a ~37B dense model would. At high load this story gets more complicated. The all-to-all pattern serializes badly under bursty traffic, and "hot experts" (routing imbalance during a particular batch) cause both compute hotspots and KV-cache hotspots on the rank that owns the popular expert. There's also a memory wall: the full 671B has to live on GPUs even if any single batch only fires ~37B of them, so VRAM pressure stays at the dense-671B level. Operators serve MoE models at lower utilization than dense models because of this — paying for empty seats so the popular ones don't bottleneck.

## One concrete advantage, one concrete disadvantage at fixed training budget

The clearest advantage is **capacity per FLOP**. Under the same training budget (FLOPs are bounded by compute), DeepSeek-V3 fits 671B worth of "knowledge slots" into the same compute envelope where a dense model would only fit ~37B. Specialization across experts gives meaningful quality wins on tasks that benefit from distributed knowledge (multilingual, math, code) without the per-token cost of a 671B dense forward pass.

The clearest disadvantage is **memory and infrastructure cost per FLOP**. The 84× larger parameter footprint demands aggressive sharding (EP for routed experts, TP for the dense attention/MLP), an all-to-all-capable fast interconnect, and load-balancing machinery (group routing, aux losses, expert capacity factors) that a dense model doesn't need. At the same dollar budget, you can train fewer total FLOPs because the *fixed* costs of an MoE training stack — engineering complexity, GPU memory for the parameter store, the cost of the interconnect — are higher. MoE wins when capacity matters more than minimizing system complexity; dense models win when you want to run on a smaller, simpler cluster.
